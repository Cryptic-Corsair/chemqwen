Intermolecular forces (non-bonded interactions) are weak attractive forces that exist between separate molecules or uncombined noble gas atoms, distinct from strong intramolecular ionic, metallic, or covalent bonds within molecules.

7.1(i) London Forces (Instantaneous Dipole–Induced Dipole / Dispersion Forces)

  • Mechanism: Electrons within an atom or non-polar molecule are in continuous, random motion. At any given instant, the electron cloud density may fluctuate unevenly across the molecule, giving rise to an instantaneous (temporary) dipole.
  • Induction: This temporary dipole distorts the electron cloud of a neighbouring molecule, inducing an induced dipole of opposite charge.
  • Attraction: The δ+ end of one temporary dipole attracts the δ− end of the neighbouring induced dipole.
  • Occurrence: London forces exist between all atoms and molecular species (whether polar or non-polar), as well as monatomic noble gases, but do not exist in giant ionic lattices.

7.1(ii) Permanent Dipole–Permanent Dipole Interactions

  • Mechanism: These forces occur between polar molecules that possess permanent dipoles resulting from differences in electronegativity across covalent bonds.
  • Attraction: Electrostatic attraction occurs between the permanent δ+ region of one molecule and the permanent δ− region of an adjacent molecule.
  • Relative Strength: For small molecules containing similar numbers of total electrons, permanent dipole–dipole interactions are generally stronger than London forces. For instance, propanone (polar) has a higher boiling temperature (56°C) than butane (non-polar, 0°C) because more energy is required to break permanent dipole forces.

7.1(iii) Hydrogen Bonds

  1. Mechanism: Hydrogen bonding is a particularly strong, specialized form of permanent dipole–permanent dipole interaction.
  2. Requirements:
    • A hydrogen atom covalently attached directly to one of the three most electronegative elements: Nitrogen (N), Oxygen (O), or Fluorine (F).
    • A lone pair of electrons on the N, O, or F atom of an adjacent molecule.
  3. Explanation: The high electronegativity difference strongly pulls shared electrons away from the hydrogen atom. Because hydrogen has no inner shell of shielding electrons, its positive nucleus is exposed, creating a high δ+ charge density that strongly attracts the lone pair on a neighbouring N, O, or F atom.
  4. Relative Strength Hierarchy:
    Covalent bonds≫ Hydrogen bonds> Permanent dipole–dipole forces> London dispersion forces

Hydrogen bonding significantly dictates the behaviour of simple hydrides containing N, O, and F:

Water (H2O)
  • Each oxygen atom has two O–H bonds and two lone pairs of electrons.
  • Water molecules can form an extensive 3D hydrogen-bonded network with up to four hydrogen bonds per molecule.
Ammonia (NH3)
  • Nitrogen forms three N–H bonds and possesses one lone pair of electrons.
  • Because it has only one lone pair, ammonia forms an average of one hydrogen bond per molecule, making its intermolecular attraction less extensive than water's.
Hydrogen Fluoride (HF)
  • Fluorine is the most electronegative element and has three lone pairs, but only one hydrogen atom per molecule.
  • HF forms long linear hydrogen-bonded chains (HF)n. Despite having strong individual hydrogen bonds, HF forms fewer hydrogen bonds per molecule overall compared to water.

Water exhibits two major anomalous physical properties directly caused by its extensive hydrogen bonding network:

7.3(i) High Melting and Boiling Temperatures

  • Observation: Water has anomalously high melting and boiling points (and enthalpy of vaporisation) compared to other Group 6 hydrides (H2S, H2Se, H2Te) and molecules of comparable molar mass.
  • Explanation: While H2S, H2Se, and H2Te are held together primarily by weaker London dispersion forces, liquid water requires a large amount of thermal energy to overcome its extensive network of strong hydrogen bonds.

7.3(ii) Lower Density of Ice Compared to Liquid Water

  • Observation: Ice floats on liquid water because solid ice at 0°C is less dense than liquid water at 0°C.
  • Explanation: In liquid water, hydrogen bonds constantly break and re-form dynamically. When water freezes into ice, the molecules are locked into a fixed, rigid 3D hexagonal ring structure held open by 4 hydrogen bonds per molecule. This arrangement creates an open, cage-like structure with large empty spaces. When ice melts, this open ring framework collapses, allowing water molecules to pack closer together, which increases the liquid's density.

Hydrogen bonding can be predicted in any compound where hydrogen is bonded to nitrogen or oxygen:

  • Alcohols (R–OH): Form intermolecular hydrogen bonds via the hydroxyl group.
  • Carboxylic Acids (R–COOH): Form hydrogen-bonded dimers in non-polar solvents.
  • Amines (R–NH2, R2–NH) & Amides (R–CONH2): Form hydrogen bonds via N–H groups and lone pairs on nitrogen.
  • Proteins & Biological Macromolecules: Rely on N–H···O=C hydrogen bonds to maintain secondary structures.

7.5(i) Boiling Temperatures of Alkanes with Increasing Chain Length

  • Trend: Boiling temperatures of unbranched alkanes increase steadily as carbon chain length increases.
  • Explanation: As chain length grows, relative molecular mass and the total number of electrons per molecule increase. Larger electron clouds undergo greater fluctuations in electron density, generating stronger instantaneous and induced dipoles. Furthermore, longer straight chains offer a larger surface area of contact between molecules, increasing total London dispersion forces and requiring more energy to separate molecules.

7.5(ii) Effect of Branching on Alkane Boiling Temperatures

  • Trend: Branched-chain alkanes have lower boiling temperatures than their unbranched structural isomers.
  • Explanation: Branching makes molecules more compact and spherical, which reduces the surface area of contact between adjacent molecules. Fewer points of contact weaken the overall London dispersion forces, requiring less thermal energy to overcome. For example, pentane boils at 309 K, 2-methylbutane at 301 K, and 2,2-dimethylpropane at 283 K.

7.5(iii) Low Volatility (Higher Boiling Points) of Alcohols Compared to Alkanes

  • Trend: Alcohols have significantly higher boiling points (lower volatility) than alkanes with similar electron numbers or chain lengths.
  • Explanation: Alkanes are non-polar and held together solely by weak London dispersion forces. Alcohols contain the polar –OH group, allowing them to form strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds in addition to London forces. Breaking these extra hydrogen bonds requires substantially higher thermal energy. For instance, propane boils at −42°C while propan-1-ol boils at 97°C.

7.5(iv) Trends in Boiling Temperatures of Hydrogen Halides (HF to HI)

  1. Observation: HF has an anomalously high boiling point (293 K). From HCl to HI, boiling points drop sharply to HCl (188 K) and then rise steadily through HBr (207 K) to HI (238 K).
  2. Explanation:
    • HF Anomaly: HF forms strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds due to the extreme polarity of the H–F bond and small, intense lone pairs on fluorine.
    • HCl to HI Trend: HCl, HBr, and HI cannot form hydrogen bonds; they experience permanent dipole–dipole forces and London forces. Going from HCl to HI, electronegativity decreases, so permanent dipole forces weaken. However, the number of electrons increases significantly (HCl < HBr < HI), strengthening the London dispersion forces enough to overcome the drop in permanent dipole attractions and increase the boiling point.

Dissolution depends on the balance of intermolecular interactions: a solute dissolves when the solute–solvent attractions are strong enough to overcome both solute–solute and solvent–solvent forces ("like dissolves like").

7.6(i) Water as a Solvent for Ionic Compounds

  • Mechanism: Giant ionic lattices are held together by strong electrostatic ionic bonds.
  • Hydration: Water is a polar solvent (δ+ H, δ− O). When an ionic solid is placed in water, the δ+ hydrogen atoms attract negative anions, while δ− oxygen atoms attract positive cations.
  • Energetics: The energy released during ion hydration (hydration enthalpy) supplies the energy needed to break apart the ionic lattice (lattice enthalpy).

7.6(ii) Water as a Solvent for Simple Alcohols

  • Mechanism: Small alcohols (methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol) are completely soluble in water because their hydroxyl (–OH) groups form hydrogen bonds with water molecules.
  • Chain Length Effect: As the non-polar hydrocarbon chain lengthens (e.g., hexanol, octanol), solubility in water decreases markedly. The non-polar alkyl chain cannot form hydrogen bonds with water and disrupts water's hydrogen-bonded network; hydrophobic London forces between long alkyl chains become predominant.

7.6(iii) Water as a Poor Solvent for Halogenoalkanes and Non-Polar Compounds

  • Explanation: Halogenoalkanes contain polar C–X bonds but lack H–F, H–O, or H–N bonds, meaning they cannot form hydrogen bonds with water.
  • Energetics: The permanent dipole–dipole forces formed between halogenoalkanes and water are too weak to break the strong hydrogen bonds between water molecules, rendering halogenoalkanes insoluble or only sparingly soluble in water.

7.6(iv) Non-Aqueous Solvents for Non-Polar Compounds

  • Explanation: Non-polar solutes (such as alkanes or iodine) readily dissolve in non-polar organic solvents (such as hexane or cyclohexane).
  • Interactions: The weak London forces broken between solute molecules and between solvent molecules are replaced by London forces of comparable strength between solute and solvent molecules.

Part 8A: Redox Chemistry

An oxidation number (or oxidation state) represents the formal charge an atom would have if all bonds in a compound were purely ionic.

Rules for Assigning Oxidation Numbers:
  1. Uncombined Elements: Any element in its uncombined elemental state has an oxidation number of 0 (e.g. H2, O2, Fe, S8 = 0).
  2. Simple Monatomic Ions: Equal to the charge on the ion (e.g. Na+ = +1, Mg2+ = +2, Fe3+ = +3, Cl− = −1).
  3. Group 1, 2, and 3 Metals in Compounds: Always +1, +2, and +3 respectively.
  4. Fluorine in Compounds: Always −1 (most electronegative element).
  5. Hydrogen in Compounds: Always +1, except in metal hydrides (e.g. NaH, CaH2) where hydrogen exists as a hydride ion (H−) with an oxidation number of −1.
  6. Oxygen in Compounds: Always −2, with three notable exceptions:
    • Peroxides (e.g. H2O2, Na2O2): Oxygen is −1.
    • Superoxides (e.g. KO2): Oxygen is −½.
    • Compounds with Fluorine (e.g. F2O where O = +2, and O2F2 where O = +1).
  7. Neutral Molecules: The sum of oxidation numbers of all atoms in a neutral molecule equals 0.
  8. Polyatomic Ions: The sum of oxidation numbers equals the overall charge on the ion (e.g. in SO42−, S = +6 and 4 × (−2) = −8, sum = −2).
  • Stock Notation: Roman numerals in parentheses indicate the positive oxidation state of an element that exhibits variable oxidation numbers.

  • Oxidising Agent: A species that gains electrons (is reduced) and causes another species to be oxidised. Its oxidation number decreases.
  • Reducing Agent: A species that donates electrons (is oxidised) and causes another species to be reduced. Its oxidation number increases.

A disproportionation reaction is a specific redox reaction where an element in a single species is simultaneously oxidised and reduced.

Key Examples:
1 Chlorine in Water:
Cl2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ HCl(aq) + HClO(aq)

Chlorine's oxidation state changes from 0 in Cl2 to −1 in HCl (reduction) and +1 in HClO (oxidation).

2 Decomposition of Hydrogen Peroxide:
2H2O2(aq) → 2H2O(l) + O2(g)

Oxygen changes from −1 in H2O2 to −2 in H2O (reduction) and 0 in O2 (oxidation).

Half-equations display explicit electron transfers for individual oxidation and reduction processes.

Constructing Half-Equations in Acidic Conditions:
  1. Balance atoms being oxidised/reduced
  2. Add H2O to balance oxygen atoms
  3. Add H+ ions to balance hydrogen atoms
  4. Add e− to balance overall electrical charges
Example: Combining Fe2+ oxidation and Cr2O72− reduction:
  • Oxidation: Fe2+ → Fe3+ + e− (multiply by 6)
  • Reduction: Cr2O72− + 14H+ + 6e− → 2Cr3+ + 7H2O
  • Overall Equation:
6Fe2+ + Cr2O72− + 14H+ → 6Fe3+ + 2Cr3+ + 7H2O

Part 8B: The Elements of Groups 1 and 2

  • Trend: First and second ionisation energies decrease down Groups 1 and 2.
  • Explanation:
    1. Atomic radius increases down the group.
    2. The number of inner filled electron shells increases, leading to increased shielding.
    3. Increased distance and shielding outweigh the increase in nuclear charge, weakening the electrostatic attraction between the nucleus and outermost valence electron(s).
  • Trend: Chemical reactivity increases down Groups 1 and 2.
  • Explanation: Groups 1 and 2 metals react by losing outer valence electrons to form positive ions (M+ or M2+). Because ionisation energies decrease down the group, less energy is required to remove valence electrons, facilitating faster and more vigorous reactions.

1. Reaction with Oxygen (O2)

  • Group 1: 4M(s) + O2(g) → 2M2O(s) (White basic solid oxides)
  • Group 2: 2M(s) + O2(g) → 2MO(s) (White basic solid oxides)
  • Special Notes:
    • Lithium forms simple oxide Li2O; Sodium forms peroxide Na2O2; Potassium forms superoxide KO2.
    • Group 2 metals burn with vivid flame colors (e.g. Mg burns with a blinding bright white flame to form MgO).

2. Reaction with Chlorine (Cl2)

  • Group 1: 2M(s) + Cl2(g) → 2MCl(s) (White ionic chlorides)
  • Group 2: M(s) + Cl2(g) → MCl2(s) (White ionic chlorides)

3. Reaction with Water (H2O)

  • Group 1: 2M(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H2(g)
  • Observations: Effervescence, metal melts into a sphere (Na, K), floats, moves rapidly across surface, disappears; solution turns alkaline. Potassium catches fire with a lilac flame.
  • Group 2: M(s) + 2H2O(l) → M(OH)2(aq/s) + H2(g)
  • Magnesium anomaly with Water vs Steam:
    • With cold water, Mg reacts extremely slowly: Mg(s) + 2H2O(l) → Mg(OH)2(s) + H2(g)
    • With steam (H2O(g)), Mg reacts vigorously, burning with a bright white flame to form solid Magnesium Oxide: Mg(s) + H2O(g) → MgO(s) + H2(g)

1. Reaction of Oxides with Water

  • Group 1: M2O(s) + H2O(l) → 2MOH(aq)
  • Group 2: MO(s) + H2O(l) → M(OH)2(aq)
  • Ionic Equation: O2−(s) + H2O(l) → 2OH−(aq)

2. Neutralisation Reactions with Dilute Acids

  • Oxides: MO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MCl2(aq) + H2O(l)
  • Hydroxides: M(OH)2(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l)
Crucial Exam Note on H2SO4: Reacting Ca(OH)2, Sr(OH)2, or Ba(OH)2 with dilute H2SO4 forms an insoluble sulfate layer (BaSO4) over the solid, preventing further reaction.

  • Definition: Thermal stability measures a compound's resistance to decomposition upon heating.
  • Trend: Thermal stability of Group 1 and 2 nitrates and carbonates increases down the group.
Mechanism (Polarising Power of Cation):
  1. Down the group, cation charge remains +1 or +2, but cation ionic radius increases.
  2. Charge density of the cation decreases down the group.
  3. Smaller cations with high charge density (e.g. Li+, Mg2+) exert a strong polarising power on large complex anions (CO32− or NO3−), distorting their electron cloud.
  4. This weakens the internal covalent C–O or N–O bonds within the anion, lowering the thermal energy required to decompose the salt.

Flame Test Procedure:
  1. Dip a clean platinum or nichrome wire into concentrated HCl.
  2. Heat wire in a non-luminous Bunsen flame to clean it until no color is imparted. (Conc. HCl converts compounds into volatile chloride salts).
  3. Dip wire in conc. HCl, pick up sample, place in flame, and observe color.

  • Bubble gas through limewater (Ca(OH)2); turns milky due to white precipitate of CaCO3.

2 Sulfate (SO42−)

  • Add dilute HCl or HNO3 (to destroy any CO32− ions that would form false precipitates), then add aqueous BaCl2 or Ba(NO3)2.

3 Ammonium (NH4+)

  • Add aqueous NaOH and warm gently.
  • Pungent ammonia gas (NH3) turns damp red litmus paper blue, or forms dense white fumes of NH4Cl when tested with glass rod dipped in conc. HCl.
  • NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq) → NH3(g) + H2O(l)

Explanations of Periodic Trends:
  1. Melting & Boiling Points (Increase Down Group): Halogens exist as non-polar diatomic molecules held by London dispersion forces. As molecular size and total electron count increase (F2: 18e− → I2: 106e−), electron cloud fluctuations increase, strengthening London forces and requiring higher thermal energy to separate molecules.
  2. Electronegativity (Decreases Down Group): Atomic radius and inner electron shielding increase down the group, weakening the electrostatic pull of the nucleus on shared bonding electrons.
  3. Reactivity & Oxidising Power (Decreases Down Group): Halogens react by gaining an electron to form 1− halide ions (X2 + 2e− → 2X−). Down the group, outer shells are further from the nucleus with greater shielding, making it harder to attract an incoming electron.

A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide ion from aqueous solution:

  • Cl2(aq) + 2Br−(aq) → 2Cl−(aq) + Br2(aq) (Solution turns yellow/orange)
  • Cl2(aq) + 2I−(aq) → 2Cl−(aq) + I2(aq) (Solution turns brown)
  • Br2(aq) + 2I−(aq) → 2Br−(aq) + I2(aq) (Solution turns brown)

Adding Cyclohexane (Organic Layer)

  • Halogens are non-polar and much more soluble in cyclohexane than in water. Adding cyclohexane and shaking separates the mixture into two layers: top organic layer shows distinct colors (Orange for Br2, Purple/Violet for I2).

  • Astatine (At2): Dark black solid, highest melting point, lowest electronegativity, weakest oxidising agent, At− is the strongest reducing agent.
